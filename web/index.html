What happens when the network breaks in only one direction?

You can call your friend. Your friend can't call you. Your phone works fine. Their phone works fine. Nothing is broken — and yet you can't have a conversation. Real networks do this constantly, and databases handle it badly.

Below are three servers trying to stay in sync. You get to break the network between them however you want, and watch what they do about it.

This is not a video, a mockup, or an animation. The actual database code — written in Go, compiled to WebAssembly — is running inside your browser tab right now. When you cut a link, you are breaking a real system and watching it genuinely fail. Nobody scripted the outcome. It is the same code the tests run against.

Why this breaks things so badly

Picture a meeting. One person can't hear anyone else, but everyone can hear them. Every ten seconds they shout "I call a new vote for chair!" — which legally forces the current chair to step down. Nobody's vote ever reaches them, so they never actually become chair. So they shout it again. And again. Forever.

Nobody in that room is broken. Nobody walked out. But no decision will ever be made again. That is a livelock, and it's exactly what a one-way network failure does to a database. It's worse than a crash, because with a crash you at least get an alert.

Databases assume servers either work or die. They have no idea what to do with a server that is half working.

An honest result, not a sales pitch: this problem already has a decent fix, invented in 2014 and shipped in etcd. It's called PreVote: before forcing a real vote, ask around informally first — "would you even vote for me?" If the answer is no, don't blow up the meeting. In our tests baseline logs 30 term bumps; PreVote logs 1. CrossFault also logs 1. So CrossFault ties here, it doesn't win. Pretending otherwise would be easy and dishonest.

So what's actually new here?

PreVote stops the broken server from causing damage. It doesn't help the server understand that it's broken, and it doesn't get any traffic through.

CrossFault keeps a map of who can reach whom, separately for each direction — because "A can reach B" and "B can reach A" are genuinely two different facts. That map buys two things:

  • A cut-off server works out that it's cut off, and deliberately stays quiet.
  • Messages take a detour through a third server when the direct route is dead — like traffic rerouting around a closed road.

The other half: someone tampering with the wires

Second problem. These servers live in different clouds, so their messages cross the public internet through equipment nobody fully controls. What if someone rewrites a message in transit — changing pay $10 into pay $10000?

The usual panic response is "we need Byzantine fault tolerance" — the heavy machinery blockchains use. It's slow, it needs three times as many servers, and here it is completely unnecessary.

Think of a wax seal on an envelope. The postman can steal your letter. They can burn it. What they cannot do is open it, rewrite it, and re-seal it convincingly — they don't have your seal. So the worst attack available is "the letter never arrived". And databases already handle lost messages perfectly well: they just send it again.

That's what happens here. Every message is signed by the server that wrote it and checked on arrival. Tampered ones fail the check and get binned. Hit corrupt a link and watch forgeries blocked climb while the saved data stays clean. A hard problem downgraded into an easy one.

Why not just use HTTPS? Because HTTPS protects each hop, and unwraps at every load balancer along the way — each of which sees the plain text. Google hit this exact wall and built ALTS for it.

What this does not fix: a server that has been fully hacked. It still owns its seal, so it can sign lies all day. Catching that needs a different technique — proving who lied, rather than preventing it. That layer is designed but not built yet, and it would be dishonest to imply otherwise.
